Where the story begins

The university had the data.
Nobody was reading it.

Ashford International University — a private university with three campuses across Southeast Asia and an enrolled population of approximately 8,400 students — had a retention problem it couldn't quite see clearly. Year-on-year dropout rates had been climbing for three consecutive years. By the time the academic year ended and the data was compiled into the annual report, the students who might have been saved had already left.

The institution had no shortage of data. The LMS recorded every login, every submission, every assessment score. The student information system held attendance records, grade histories, financial aid statuses, and programme enrolment data. The library system tracked resource access. The student services team logged every welfare referral and support interaction. But none of these systems spoke to each other. Each department reported its own numbers to its own leadership. Nobody had a cross-system view of the student who was quietly failing across all of them simultaneously.

The Vice-Chancellor's office commissioned a learning analytics initiative with a specific brief: build a system that identifies at-risk students early enough for the institution to intervene — not after the semester is over. The L&D and Institutional Effectiveness team was tasked with leading it.

🧠
Remember this "The institution's problem was not a data problem. It was a visibility problem. The data existed — in six separate systems, in six separate formats, read by six separate teams, none of whom could see what the others were seeing."
2
The discovery process

Before building anything,
understand what matters.

I began with a structured discovery phase across three workstreams — run concurrently to compress the timeline without sacrificing the depth of understanding the project required.

1

Stakeholder mapping and interviews

I ran structured interviews with 14 stakeholders across six departments — academic registry, student services, faculty representatives, financial aid, the library, and the Vice-Chancellor's office. The purpose was not to ask what data they wanted on a dashboard. It was to ask what decisions they needed to make, and what information was currently making those decisions harder than it needed to be. That question produced a very different answer than a requirements gathering exercise would have.

2

Data audit across six systems

I mapped every data source the institution held — LMS (Moodle), Student Information System (SIS), library access logs, financial aid records, attendance tracking, and the student services CRM. For each source I assessed: what data is captured, how frequently it is updated, what format it is stored in, and whether it contains a consistent student identifier that could serve as a linking key across systems. The audit took eight working days and surfaced two significant problems: attendance data was entered manually by faculty and had a 23% incompleteness rate, and three of the six systems used different student ID formats, requiring a reconciliation mapping before any integration was possible.

3

Risk factor research and indicator design

I reviewed published research on student attrition in higher education to identify the indicators with the strongest predictive validity for dropout risk. The evidence consistently pointed to five leading indicators: LMS engagement frequency (declining logins in the first four weeks is the single strongest early predictor), assignment submission rate (missing two or more consecutive assessments triples dropout risk), attendance patterns, cumulative GPA trend (not just current GPA but trajectory), and financial aid status changes. These five became the foundation of the risk scoring model.

💡
Design decision The decision to ground the risk model in published research rather than institutional intuition was deliberate. Several faculty members had strong views about what predicted student failure — attendance, primarily. The research showed that LMS engagement was a stronger early predictor than attendance, because students typically stop engaging digitally before they stop attending physically. Building the model on evidence rather than opinion was what gave it credibility with the academic community.
3
The analytics framework

A risk score every academic
could understand and act on.

The analytics framework had to do two things simultaneously: be rigorous enough to be statistically defensible, and be simple enough that a faculty member with no data background could look at a student's profile and know immediately what to do. Those two requirements are frequently in tension. Resolving that tension was the central design challenge.

I designed a composite risk score — the Student Progression Index (SPI) — built from the five evidence-based indicators identified in the discovery phase. Each indicator was weighted according to its predictive strength in the literature, then normalised to a 0–100 scale. Students scoring below 40 were flagged as high risk, 40–65 as moderate risk, and above 65 as on track.

The SPI was designed to update weekly — automatically, as new LMS, attendance, and submission data flowed in — so that a student's risk profile reflected their current trajectory rather than their historical average. A student who had been performing well but missed three consecutive LMS logins and two submissions in week six would see their SPI drop immediately, triggering an alert to their personal tutor before the end of that week.

💡
The naming decision I deliberately named the score the "Student Progression Index" rather than an "At-Risk Score." The framing mattered enormously for faculty adoption. A score labelled "at-risk" positions the student as a problem to be managed. A "progression index" positions the score as a measure of forward movement — something to support, not flag. Several faculty members cited this framing as the reason they trusted the tool enough to use it.

The Power BI dashboard

Four views.
One source of truth.

The four views served distinct audiences and decisions. The Institution View — shown above — gave the Vice-Chancellor's office and academic leadership a weekly snapshot of overall student progression, risk distribution by school, and intervention activity. The School View gave deans and heads of department a programme-level breakdown, enabling them to identify whether risk was clustered in a specific cohort, module, or academic period. The Personal Tutor View gave individual tutors a prioritised list of their own tutees flagged for follow-up — with the specific indicators that triggered the flag and a log of previous interventions. The Student Services View gave the welfare and counselling team a feed of students who had been referred or self-referred, with cross-system context that had previously required manual collation from four separate systems.

💡
A critical design choice — what not to show I made a deliberate decision to exclude financial aid status from the student-facing and tutor-facing views, and to restrict it to the student services view only. Surfacing financial vulnerability to academic staff without context or training would have created welfare risks rather than reducing them. The data was available — the decision was not to display it to audiences who were not equipped to respond to it appropriately.
5
What made this hard

Four challenges.
Four resolutions.

Challenge
The attendance data was 23% incomplete. Faculty were entering attendance manually into the SIS — but compliance was inconsistent, particularly among part-time and visiting lecturers. With nearly a quarter of attendance records missing, using attendance as a risk indicator would have produced unreliable scores and — more dangerously — given a false "on track" reading to students who were actually disengaging.
Resolution
I adjusted the weighting of attendance in the SPI model to account for its incompleteness — reducing its contribution to the composite score and increasing the weighting of LMS engagement data, which was captured automatically and had 100% coverage. I also flagged the attendance data quality issue formally to the academic registry, with the recommendation that the manual entry process be replaced with a QR-based digital attendance system. That recommendation was acted on in the following semester.
Challenge
Faculty resistance to the SPI model. Several senior academics pushed back on the idea of a composite risk score, arguing that student performance was too nuanced to be reduced to a single number and that the score would lead to formulaic responses rather than genuine pastoral care. One professor of humanities described it as "turning students into KPIs."
Resolution
I addressed this directly in the faculty briefing sessions by reframing the SPI as a prompt, not a prescription. The score didn't tell a tutor what to do — it told them who to call first. The underlying indicator breakdown was always visible alongside the score, so a tutor could see exactly which signals were driving the flag and make their own judgement about what was happening. I also invited two of the most vocal critics to review the first month of live data alongside their own pastoral knowledge — in every case, the students flagged by the model were students the tutors had already been quietly worried about. That validation shifted the conversation from resistance to engagement.
Challenge
Three systems used different student ID formats. The LMS used a numeric ID, the library system used an alphanumeric format prefixed by campus code, and the student services CRM used a separate welfare reference number. Without a consistent linking key, cross-system data could not be joined reliably — and an incorrect join would have attributed one student's data to another's risk profile.
Resolution
I worked with the IT department to build a reconciliation mapping table — a master reference that linked all three ID formats to the SIS student number, which was the only identifier present across all six systems. The mapping table required a one-time manual verification of 600 edge cases where automated matching was ambiguous. This added five days to the build timeline but eliminated the data integrity risk entirely. The mapping table was then maintained automatically as new students were enrolled.
Challenge
PDPA and data privacy compliance. The dashboard aggregated personally identifiable student data across six systems — including academic performance, welfare referrals, and financial aid status. Several faculty members raised concerns about whether displaying this data in a shared platform complied with the Personal Data Protection Act and the institution's own data governance policies.
Resolution
I worked with the institution's data protection officer and legal counsel to document the legal basis for processing under PDPA — specifically the legitimate interest basis for student welfare, with appropriate safeguards. The dashboard was built with role-based access controls: tutors could only see their own tutees, deans could only see their school, and sensitive welfare and financial data was restricted to the student services view. A data processing record was added to the institution's register before go-live. The DPO subsequently cited the project as a model for responsible data use in institutional analytics.

The number that mattered most to the institution was not on the dashboard — it was the one calculated at the end of the academic year. 19% fewer students withdrew compared to the prior year. For a university of 8,400 students, that represented a significant financial and reputational improvement, but more importantly it represented students who completed their degrees rather than carrying the weight of an incomplete qualification.

The dashboard also changed how the institution talked about student progression. Before the project, attrition was discussed in retrospect — at the end of semester reviews, with historical data and post-hoc explanations. After the project, it was discussed in real time, with forward-looking data and specific intervention plans. The conversation moved from "why did these students leave?" to "what are we going to do about these students this week?"

"For the first time, I could see which of my students needed a call before they stopped coming to class. The dashboard didn't replace the conversation — it told me who to have it with."

— Personal Tutor, School of Business, Ashford International University

An undesigned outcome emerged six months into the project: faculty began using the dashboard not just for pastoral intervention but for curriculum review. When the School of Humanities showed consistently lower SPI scores than other schools, the dean used the data to open a conversation about workload distribution and assessment scheduling — identifying that three major assignments were due in the same week for second-year students, creating a structural pressure point the dashboard made visible for the first time.

🧠
Remember this "The dashboard did not save a single student on its own. It gave the people who could save students the information they needed to act — at the moment it was still possible to act. The analytics was never the intervention. The human conversation was."
