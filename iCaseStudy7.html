1
Where the story begins

The system was live. The people were not ready.

A financial services organisation had completed a major technology transformation — replacing three legacy finance systems with Oracle Financials Cloud, Oracle's integrated suite covering General Ledger, Accounts Payable, Accounts Receivable, and Fixed Assets. The go-live date was fixed and immovable. And the training programme, which should have started six weeks earlier, was still unbuilt.

When I came on board as Lead ID, there were 400 users across four locations and two countries who had never seen the system. Oracle University's generic cloud training had been dismissed early as too broad for the organisation's configured workflows — the correct assessment, but it had left a training vacuum with a four-month window to fill it.

The content audit finding that changed the design strategy: I reviewed 340 pages of Oracle configuration documentation and conducted structured interviews with 18 SMEs across six user groups. What emerged was the central insight: the errors users were most likely to make were not navigation errors — they were business process errors. An AP clerk who didn't understand the three-way match between a purchase order, goods receipt, and supplier invoice would misprocess invoices regardless of how well they navigated the Oracle Payables dashboard.

This shifted the entire instructional strategy: teach the Oracle process first — Procure-to-Pay, Order-to-Cash, Record-to-Report — then the Oracle system. Every video, every Show Me, every Try Me was sequenced accordingly.

🧠
Remember this "Oracle Financials Cloud is built around integrated process flows — Procure-to-Pay, Order-to-Cash, Record-to-Report. Every transaction a user enters has upstream dependencies and downstream consequences. Training that focuses only on system navigation produces users who can enter data. Training that focuses on process flow produces users who understand what they are entering, why it matters, and what happens if they get it wrong."
2
The learning architecture

Six Oracle role-based pathways. One process-first principle.

The programme was built as a four-layer learning architecture applied consistently across all six Oracle Financials Cloud role pathways — each pathway mapped to a specific user group and the Oracle modules they would operate daily.

The six role-based pathways covered: GL Accountant (Oracle General Ledger — journal entry, period-end close, account reconciliation, OTBI reporting), AP Clerk (Oracle Payables — invoice processing, three-way match, payment runs), AR Manager (Oracle Receivables — customer invoicing, cash application, collections), Fixed Asset Controller (Oracle Fixed Assets — asset additions, depreciation, retirements), Procurement Officer (Oracle Procurement — purchase requisitions, purchase orders, Procure-to-Pay cycle), and Finance Business Partner (OTBI dashboards, Financial Reporting Studio, period-end management reporting).

Each pathway followed the same four-stage learning sequence:

📋
Four-stage architecture — applied across all six pathways Stage 1 — Oracle Process Explainer Video: A produced video (5–8 min) establishing the Oracle business process context before any system navigation. The AP pathway opened with the three-way match concept — what it is, why Oracle enforces it, and what a mismatch means for the organisation's financial controls — before a single Payables screen appeared.

Stage 2 — Show Me (Oracle Guided Demonstration): Screen-recorded walkthroughs of the organisation's configured Oracle Financials Cloud environment — not Oracle's generic demo environment. Narration focused on decision points: why this field, what happens if this Oracle approval is bypassed, how this transaction flows downstream into GL.

Stage 3 — Try Me (Oracle Simulation): Interactive simulations where learners completed Oracle transactions independently — with decreasing support across three levels: field-by-field guidance → task-level prompts → unsupported (replicating the go-live environment).

Stage 4 — Scenario-Based Assessment: Every assessment scenario was drawn directly from real Oracle error patterns the SME interviews had identified. An AP clerk scenario presented an invoice with a quantity variance against the Oracle purchase order and asked the user to identify the correct Oracle workflow response.
💡
The must-know filter — 38% scope reduction Applying a must-know / need-to-know / nice-to-know filter to each Oracle pathway against the configuration documentation reduced scope by 38% — making four-month delivery genuinely achievable without compromising the training standard. Every screen cut was a screen that would not have changed Oracle performance on the job.

Your STAR answer

The full story. In your words.

Use this for: "Tell me about a time you designed and delivered training for a large-scale ERP implementation." Also works for: "Tell me about a time you identified that the real problem wasn't what the client thought it was."

S
Situation
A financial services organisation had just completed an Oracle Financials Cloud implementation — replacing three legacy finance systems with Oracle's integrated suite covering General Ledger, Accounts Payable, Accounts Receivable, Fixed Assets, and Procurement. The go-live date was fixed and immovable. When I came on board as Lead ID, there were 400 users across four locations and two countries who had never seen the system — and no training programme in place. Oracle University's generic cloud training had already been dismissed as too broad for the organisation's configured workflows, leaving a training vacuum with four months to fill it.
T
Task
As Lead ID, I was responsible for designing and delivering six bespoke Oracle role-based training pathways — covering GL accountants, AP clerks, AR managers, Fixed Asset controllers, Procurement officers, and Finance Business Partners — and producing a go-live readiness dashboard that would give senior leadership the evidence they needed to approve Oracle go-live. Every pathway had to be completed and assessed before go-live day. I led a team of ten instructional designers through the full build.
A
Action — your thinking, your decisions
Before building anything, I ran a two-week discovery phase. I reviewed 340 pages of Oracle Financials Cloud configuration documentation — chart of accounts, approval hierarchies, workflow configurations — and conducted structured interviews with 18 SMEs across the six Oracle user groups. The question I was asking was not "what Oracle functionality do you need to train?" It was "what decisions do users in this role make every day, and what do they need to understand — about the Oracle business process, not just the Oracle interface — to make those decisions correctly?"

What those conversations revealed changed the entire design strategy. The errors users were most likely to make were not navigation errors — they were Oracle business process errors. An AP clerk who didn't understand the three-way match between a purchase order, goods receipt, and supplier invoice would misprocess invoices regardless of how well they navigated the Payables dashboard. A GL accountant who didn't understand the period-end close sequence would create manual journal entries that should have been generated automatically by Oracle's Subledger Accounting engine — creating reconciliation problems that would persist for months.

I restructured the entire programme around a process-first principle. Each Oracle pathway began with the relevant Oracle process flow — Procure-to-Pay for AP and Procurement, Order-to-Cash for AR, Record-to-Report for GL — before any Oracle interface navigation. I applied a must-know/need-to-know filter that reduced scope by 38%, making four-month delivery achievable without compromising the standard.

I then directed my team of ten through a four-layer architecture: produced Oracle process explainer videos (42 videos across all pathways), Show Me guided demonstrations of the organisation's configured Oracle environment, Try Me simulations at three support levels, and scenario-based assessments drawn directly from the Oracle error patterns the SME interviews had surfaced. When the Oracle implementation team made configuration changes mid-project — approval hierarchy and chart of accounts changes — I introduced a screen freeze policy and a rapid-rebuild workflow that kept affected Show Me modules current within 48 hours of a confirmed change.

I also introduced a differentiated entry point for the two user populations: a diagnostic pre-assessment routing users to an extended Oracle process context module if they scored below threshold — ensuring the India-based processing team (who had operated a largely manual legacy system) received the Oracle business process foundation they needed before any system navigation.
R
Result
100% of 400+ users completed their Oracle pathway before go-live — the first time in the organisation's history that a major system rollout achieved full pre-go-live training completion. 96% first-attempt pass rate across all six Oracle role-based pathways. 62% reduction in support desk queries in the first 30 days post go-live compared to the organisation's previous system rollout. The go-live readiness dashboard showed role-specific Oracle competency scores — not just completion flags — giving the project sponsor granular, defensible evidence of user readiness before activating the live Oracle Financials Cloud environment.
🧠
Remember this "Oracle Financials Cloud is an integrated system. GL, AP, AR, Fixed Assets, and Procurement do not operate independently — every transaction has upstream dependencies and downstream consequences. Training that ignores this produces users who can navigate Oracle but cannot operate it. The distinction matters enormously on go-live day."

Be ready for pushback

The tough questions. Answer them with confidence.

Interviewers — particularly at Aptara — will probe your Oracle decisions specifically. Here are the questions most likely to come up.

Oracle Process — Design Decisions

Oracle University teaches the standard, unconfigured Oracle product. Every Oracle implementation configures the system differently — the chart of accounts, approval hierarchies, supplier data, and workflow configurations are unique to each organisation. End users trained on Oracle's generic product encounter a system that looks and behaves differently from the one they were trained on.

But the deeper problem is structural. Oracle Financials Cloud is built around integrated process flows — Procure-to-Pay, Order-to-Cash, Record-to-Report. Every Oracle transaction a user enters is an act within a business process. An AP clerk who doesn't understand the three-way match will trigger invoice holds they don't know how to resolve, regardless of how well they can navigate the Payables dashboard. A GL accountant who doesn't understand which journal entries Oracle's Subledger Accounting engine creates automatically versus which require manual intervention will create duplicate entries that corrupt the trial balance.

System-first design teaches users to click correctly. Process-first design teaches users to think correctly. When the Oracle interface changes — and in Oracle Cloud, it changes every quarter — a user trained process-first can adapt. A user trained system-first cannot. The 62% reduction in support desk queries in the first 30 days post go-live is the evidence.

Oracle screen recordings show what Oracle does. Produced Oracle process explainer videos explain why Oracle does what it does — and why the underlying financial process demands it.

A screen recording of an Oracle Payables approval workflow shows the clicks. A produced video explains what an Oracle AP approval actually authorises, what a three-way match exception means for the organisation's financial controls, and how the approval decision connects downstream to the Oracle payment run and GL posting. That process context is what makes the subsequent Oracle Show Me meaningful rather than mechanical.

The speed argument underestimates the production system we built: a modular Storyline component library with standardised Oracle interaction patterns, a pre-approved script template, and a rapid-build workflow. A video could go from first-draft script to finished output in five working days. That pace made 42 Oracle process videos viable within a four-month window.

On two levels — governance and architecture.

At the governance level, I negotiated a configuration freeze agreement with the Oracle implementation lead — a formally agreed date after which no changes to training-relevant Oracle configuration (approval hierarchies, chart of accounts segment values, workflow routing rules) would be made without a documented change request and a formal impact assessment on the training content.

At the architecture level, I built the Oracle Show Me modules using a modular Storyline component library — standardised Oracle interaction patterns and screen templates that could be updated individually without rebuilding entire modules. A confirmed Oracle configuration change could be reflected in affected Show Me modules within 48 hours. This made the change request process acceptable to the implementation team — they knew the training impact was bounded and manageable.

The lesson: in Oracle training projects, you cannot control the implementation timeline, but you can design your production architecture to absorb the impact of late configuration changes without crisis.

Because Oracle Financials Cloud presents different interfaces and different functions to different users based on their role-based security model. A GL accountant's Oracle environment looks and behaves completely differently from an AP clerk's — different dashboards, different transaction types, different approval responsibilities, different reporting access.

A generic Oracle programme would have trained every user in every workflow — including Oracle workflows irrelevant to their role. In a system where data entry errors have real financial consequences, training people in Oracle processes they will never use creates confusion, not competence. It also wastes the most valuable resource in a pre-go-live window: learner attention.

The six Oracle pathways were also more efficient than they appear: many modules were shared across roles, with role-specific annotation layers applied to the same core Oracle content. The 42 process videos served six pathways — the average video was used by multiple Oracle user groups, not built exclusively for one.

Oracle Module Knowledge

The three-way match is Oracle Payables' core control mechanism for verifying that a supplier invoice should be paid. Oracle AP automatically matches three documents: the Purchase Order (what the organisation agreed to buy, at what price, from which supplier), the Goods Receipt (confirmation that the goods were actually received in the correct quantity and condition), and the Supplier Invoice (the supplier's request for payment).

Oracle automatically places a hold on any invoice where there is a discrepancy — a quantity variance, a price variance, or a missing goods receipt. The hold prevents payment until the discrepancy is resolved.

It is the most important AP training concept because it is the most common source of post-go-live support queries. An AP clerk who doesn't understand the three-way match will call the help desk every time they encounter an Oracle invoice hold — they know something is wrong but don't know what the correct resolution is. In the CS7 project, the AP pathway opened with the three-way match concept before any Oracle Payables screen was shown. This single design decision was responsible for a significant portion of the 62% support query reduction.

The Oracle period-end close is the process of finalising all accounting transactions for a given month — ensuring all Oracle subledger transactions have been processed (AP invoices cleared, AR receipts applied, Fixed Asset depreciation run), all manual journal entries posted, all account reconciliations completed, and the Oracle accounting period formally closed so no further transactions can be posted to it.

It is a training challenge for three reasons. First, it requires coordination across multiple Oracle user roles — an AP clerk's failure to process all invoices before the Oracle close deadline blocks the GL accountant's ability to close the period. Training that covers each Oracle role in isolation misses this critical interdependency. Second, Oracle period-end errors are consequential — an incorrect journal posted to the wrong Oracle period cannot be deleted, only corrected through a reversing entry. Third, the Oracle close sequence is organisation-specific — different close calendars, different cut-off policies, different Oracle approval hierarchies.

In the CS7 project, the GL accountant pathway addressed the period-end close in detail — specifically the sequence of steps across Oracle GL, AP, AR, and Fixed Assets that must be completed before the Oracle period can be locked. This was the most complex module in the entire programme.

The methodology transfers completely — the specific Oracle interface knowledge does not, but the instructional design principles are platform-independent.

Process-first design is as essential for SAP S/4HANA as it is for Oracle Financials Cloud. SAP's Procure-to-Pay, Order-to-Cash, and Record-to-Report process flows are conceptually identical to Oracle's — the transactions have different names (SAP uses "MIRO" for invoice verification where Oracle uses the Payables import), but the business logic is the same. A user who doesn't understand why SAP enforces a three-way match will make the same errors as an Oracle user who doesn't understand it.

Role-based pathway design, the must-know/need-to-know content filter, the Show Me / Try Me / scenario assessment architecture, the configuration freeze governance model, the go-live readiness dashboard — all of these apply to SAP, Workday, Microsoft Dynamics, or any enterprise system training project. The platform changes. The methodology doesn't.

Measurement & Evidence

The scenario-based assessments were designed from the outset to serve a dual purpose: learning reinforcement and Oracle go-live readiness evidence. Each Oracle assessment scenario produced a role-specific competency score — not a pass/fail completion flag — showing which Oracle transactions the user had demonstrated proficiency in and which required additional support.

The readiness dashboard aggregated these scores by Oracle role and by location. Rather than a single number — "87% completion rate" — the dashboard showed, for example, that AP clerks in the India office had demonstrated proficiency in Oracle invoice entry and supplier enquiry but showed lower scores on Oracle payment exception handling. This triggered a targeted follow-up session on that specific Oracle topic before go-live.

The project sponsor used this dashboard to approve Oracle go-live — not a completion percentage, but granular Oracle competency evidence. That is the difference between a dashboard that shows training has happened and a dashboard that shows Oracle readiness.

Three things, in priority order.

First, a Digital Adoption Platform — specifically WalkMe integrated with the Oracle environment. The Show Me / Try Me architecture was effective for pre-go-live certification. But in the first weeks after Oracle go-live, users encounter real transactions that don't match training scenarios exactly. WalkMe in-application guidance would support those moments — the training builds the Oracle mental model; the DAP supports the first real execution in the live Oracle environment.

Second, spaced reinforcement at 7, 21, and 60 days post Oracle go-live. The forgetting curve is a real risk in ERP training — users certified in week three of a four-week window may have forgotten critical Oracle process steps by month two. Targeted microlearning nudges linked to each user's specific Oracle assessment gaps would address this.

Third, a cross-functional Oracle simulation — a joint session for GL accountants, AP managers, and AR managers working through the period-end close sequence together. The period-end close requires coordination across Oracle modules, and the training delivered each role in relative isolation. That isolation was the weakest link in the overall programme — the post-go-live support data showed most period-end queries came from role-boundary confusion rather than Oracle navigation errors.
