About You
I am an instructional designer and learning consultant with 23 years of experience working across two core practice areas — pre-sales learning consultancy, where I help organisations translate business challenges into defined learning solutions, and the end-to-end design and development of those solutions. My work spans eLearning, ILT, virtual ILT, business simulations, gamified assessments, and AI-powered performance support tools, across financial services, healthcare, FMCG, pharma, manufacturing, humanitarian, and higher education sectors. What has remained constant across all of it is a single principle: I work with the end in mind. For me, that means designing learning that produces measurable change — not just activity.
Possible follow-up
"Has there been a situation where things didn't go as planned or you weren't able to produce measurable change?"
Yes — and I think anyone who claims otherwise either hasn't been doing this long enough or isn't being honest.

Early in my career, I designed a soft skills programme for a mid-size organisation — well-structured, well-received, strong reaction scores. Participants liked it. Managers thought it was useful. We called it a success.

About six months later I had an honest conversation with the HR head, who told me that she couldn't point to a single thing that had changed on the floor. Behaviour was the same. The problem the programme was supposed to address was still there.

What had gone wrong was straightforward in hindsight: I had designed for the training room, not for the workplace. There was no transfer mechanism — no manager briefing before the programme, no on-the-job application structure after it, no accountability for whether anyone actually tried the new behaviours in a real situation. I had built a good learning experience and a poor performance intervention. Those are two very different things.

That failure changed how I design. Every programme I build now has a transfer architecture — something that bridges what happens in the room and what happens back at the desk. I am a better designer because that happened. And I would rather tell you that story than pretend it didn't.

What these three have in common is that the measurement was agreed before the design began, not retrofitted after delivery. That is the only way measurement is credible.

Strengths & Weaknesses
Resilience — and the adaptability that comes with it.

Over 23 years I have worked across large consultancies, mid-size agencies, in-house teams, and for the last year and a half, independently. Each environment required a different way of working. Adapting across those contexts — without losing the thread of who I am as a designer — is something I have had to develop deliberately.

The projects have tested that resilience in their own ways too. Briefs that changed mid-build. Go-live dates that would not move. Source material that turned out to be unusable. Each time, the work got done — not because the conditions were favourable, but because I have learned to work well precisely when they are not.

That resilience is not stubbornness. It is the ability to stay focused on the outcome when everything around the project is in flux.
I have a tendency to invest more time in the discovery and diagnosis phase than clients sometimes expect. I ask a lot of questions before I start designing — which occasionally creates friction with stakeholders who want to move quickly to production. Over the years I have learned to manage this better — framing the discovery phase as a risk management step rather than a delay, and showing clients early on how the questions I am asking will save time and rework later. But the underlying instinct — to understand before I build — is one I have never wanted to lose entirely, because it is also the thing that produces the best work.
Through active practice rather than passive consumption. The AI and learning analytics capabilities I have developed over the last two years came from building live projects — a Microsoft Copilot Studio virtual assistant for a pharma sales team, an LXP integration using xAPI for a humanitarian organisation, and a Power BI learning analytics dashboard for a higher education institution. I also follow research from the Learning and Performance Institute, ATD, and key academic journals on instructional design and adult learning. But my honest answer is that the most reliable way I stay current is by taking on projects that push me into territory I have not worked in before — and treating each one as a learning engagement as much as a delivery engagement.
Domain & Craft
My primary authoring tool is Articulate Storyline, which I have used across the full range of eLearning formats — linear modules, scenario-based branching, software simulations, and gamified assessments. I have also worked with Articulate Rise 360 for rapid responsive development, Adobe Captivate for software training, Vyond for animated explainer content, and iSpring for rapid development. On the infrastructure side I have designed and deployed content across multiple LMS platforms, built xAPI-compliant courses tracked through Learning Record Stores, and integrated eLearning content with LXP environments. More recently I have been working with Microsoft Copilot Studio to build AI-powered performance support tools, and with Power BI to build learning analytics dashboards. My approach to tools has always been to let the performance context drive the technology choice — not the other way around.
A TNA always starts with a performance gap — but that gap has more than one origin. Sometimes it surfaces as a problem: error rates climbing, customer complaints increasing, a compliance failure that should not have happened. Sometimes it surfaces as a strategic opportunity: the organisation is moving in a new direction and needs capabilities it does not yet have. Both are legitimate triggers, and both deserve a different type of investigation.

At the organisational level, I work with senior leadership to understand where the business is heading and what the strategy requires that the current workforce cannot yet deliver. This is less about training and more about capability forecasting — what does the organisation need to be able to do in 12 to 18 months that it cannot do today?

At the functional level, the gap usually surfaces through data that already exists — quality metrics, error rates, customer feedback, manager observations, process audits. The TNA's job is not to discover this data from scratch but to formalise and validate what the organisation is already seeing informally, and to connect it to a specific performance standard that the training can be designed to close.

At the individual level, I am cautious about relying on self-assessments — particularly when the data is anywhere near the appraisal cycle. People do not rate themselves accurately under those conditions, and the data becomes unreliable. I prefer structured observation, work sample reviews, or short skills challenges that surface the gap directly rather than asking someone to declare it. Where I do use manager assessments, I run calibration sessions first — to ensure that different managers are applying the same standard, not just their own interpretation of it.

The output is always the same: a priority gap register that shows what needs to change, by role, by level of urgency, and linked to the business outcome the training is expected to support.
My approach to measuring effectiveness depends on the context — and I deliberately choose the framework based on what the client actually needs to know and what decision the data will inform.

For most programmes, I start with Kirkpatrick as a structural guide — but I apply it selectively. Level 1 reaction data rarely tells me anything useful, so I do not make it the headline metric. Where I focus most of my energy is Level 3 — are people actually doing things differently on the job? I build post-programme observation frameworks or manager verification structures into the design to capture this, rather than leaving it to an end-of-year survey.

Where a client needs to make a financial case for learning investment, I bring in the Phillips ROI methodology — converting Level 4 business impact data into a cost-benefit ratio. I used this approach on an ERP rollout where the 62% reduction in support desk queries after go-live gave the L&D team a clear, defensible return on the training investment.

When I need a faster read on whether learning is transferring — without a full evaluation study — I use Brinkerhoff's Success Case Method. I identify the learners who applied the learning well and those who did not, compare them, and surface what is enabling or blocking transfer.

And increasingly, I connect learning data directly to business KPIs through xAPI tracking and Power BI dashboards — which moves measurement from a post-programme report into a live, ongoing view of whether the learning is producing the outcomes it was designed to produce.

The framework is always a means to an end. The question I am always trying to answer is the same one regardless of which model I use: did something actually change?
I distinguish carefully between gamification — applying game mechanics to a learning experience — and game-based learning, which involves building an actual game. My experience is with gamification. The most significant example is an IPC compliance assessment I designed for a healthcare organisation, where the previous version had produced only a 58% compliance-level performance rate. I designed a Care Credits mechanic — a cumulative consequence system across six clinical ward environments — where every incorrect decision cost Credits that carried forward across the entire assessment, mirroring the clinical reality that IPC failures compound. The result was a 91% perfect score rate — compared to 58% on the prior version. The insight was not about making it fun. It was about making the consequences feel real in a safe environment. That is what gamification, done properly, achieves.
The fundamental difference is that virtual delivery strips away the ambient social pressure that keeps people engaged in a room — the eye contact, the peer awareness, the physical presence of a facilitator. Without deliberate design to replace those mechanisms, virtual learning defaults to passive watching. My approach is to build a structured interaction point within every ten minutes of content — Zoom polls before teaching rather than after, chat waterfall techniques that capture first-instinct thinking before groupthink forms, breakout rooms with specific tasks rather than open discussion, and role-play pairs with clear observation frameworks. I also design the facilitator guide very differently for virtual delivery — specifying the exact platform management instructions alongside the instructional moves, because a technically uncertain facilitator loses the room immediately in a virtual environment.
With the assumption that my defaults are wrong until proven otherwise. I have designed learning for audiences across Southeast Asia, the Middle East, Europe, North America, and sub-Saharan Africa — and the most consistent lesson across all of them is that the instructional conventions I might take for granted in one context are either invisible or counterproductive in another. Practically, this means involving regional SMEs and target audience representatives in the review process, building flexibility into scenarios so they can be localised without rebuilding the architecture, and being conservative about humour, idiom, and cultural reference. I have also designed programmes that had to function on low-bandwidth connections across 28 countries — which taught me a great deal about designing for constraint rather than for optimal conditions.
My most substantial analytics project was a learning analytics framework and Power BI dashboard for an international university, integrating data across six previously siloed systems — LMS, Student Information System, library access logs, financial aid records, attendance tracking, and a student services CRM. I designed a Student Progression Index — a composite risk score built from five evidence-based indicators, updated weekly from live system data — that identified at-risk students early enough for tutors to intervene. 347 high-risk students were identified and contacted in the first semester. 83% of those who received an intervention completed the semester. The institution's overall retention improved by 19% year-on-year — the first improvement in three years. The project also taught me that data governance and stakeholder trust are as important as the technical build — the analytics only created value because tutors trusted the model enough to act on it.
Stakeholders
I treat disagreement as a diagnostic opportunity rather than a negotiation. If a client pushes back on a design recommendation, my first response is to understand what is driving the pushback — is it a budget concern, a timeline constraint, a cultural preference, or a genuine belief that my approach is wrong? In most cases it is one of the first three, and those can be addressed directly. Where the client genuinely believes the approach is wrong, I present the evidence for my recommendation — research, precedent from similar projects, or a prototype — and then make the decision transparent: here is what the data suggests, here is what you are asking for, and here is what the likely outcome of each approach is. I have rarely lost a client to that conversation. I have occasionally been overruled — and in a few of those cases, the client's instinct turned out to be right.
The most important thing I have learned about difficult stakeholders is that resistance is almost always about framing, not substance. When a department head at a hospitality group I was working with refused to participate in a TNA — describing it as "another HR exercise that won't change anything" — I did not try to persuade him with process arguments. I reframed the conversation entirely: instead of asking for an interview about training, I asked for 30 minutes to understand what was making his team's performance harder than it needed to be. That framing worked immediately. He subsequently provided some of the richest input in the entire TNA. The lesson: connect the conversation to what the stakeholder cares about — their results, not your process.
Leadership & Process
One of the most complex projects I have led was an Oracle Financials Cloud end-user training programme for a financial services organisation — 400 users, six role-based pathways, a fixed go-live deadline, and a team of ten instructional designers working under my direction. Before building anything, I ran a content and capability audit — reviewing 340 pages of Oracle configuration documentation and conducting structured interviews with 18 SMEs. What those conversations revealed changed the entire design strategy: the errors users were most likely to make were not about navigating Oracle's interface — they were about not understanding the underlying financial process the system was built to support. I restructured the entire programme around that insight. The result was 100% pathway completion before go-live, a 96% first-attempt pass rate, and a 62% reduction in support desk queries in the first 30 days — the first time in the organisation's history that a major system rollout had achieved full pre-go-live training completion.
By impact and dependency, in that order. Impact — which project, if delayed, causes the greatest downstream consequence for the client or the business? Dependency — which project is blocking other people's work if I do not deliver my component on time? Everything else — personal preference, ease of execution, interest level — comes after those two filters. Practically, I maintain a live project dashboard that shows the status of every deliverable, every stakeholder dependency, and every deadline across all active projects. That visibility means I am managing by exception rather than by memory — I do not need to keep everything in my head because it is all visible in one place.
Through standards, not supervision. When I lead a team of instructional designers, I do not review everything — I build a shared design framework that makes good decisions the default. That means establishing agreed templates for storyboards and scripts, a must-know/need-to-know/nice-to-know content filter applied at the outline stage before anything is built, and a structured SME review protocol that gives reviewers specific accuracy gates to respond to rather than open-ended feedback. I also hold a design principle review at the start of every project — making sure every team member understands the instructional rationale behind the choices we are making, not just the production requirements. Quality comes from shared understanding, not from checking other people's work.

Aptara Specific — Senior IDM Role
A financial services organisation had replaced three legacy finance systems with Oracle Financials Cloud — covering General Ledger, Accounts Payable, Accounts Receivable, and Fixed Assets. The go-live date was fixed. When I came on as Lead ID, there were 400 users across four locations and two countries with no training programme in place — not a poor one, none at all. I had four months.

Before building anything, I ran a two-week discovery phase — reviewing 340 pages of Oracle configuration documentation and conducting structured interviews with 18 SMEs across six user groups. The key insight from those interviews changed the entire design strategy: the errors users were most likely to make were not Oracle navigation errors — they were business process errors. An AP clerk who did not understand the three-way match principle would not know why an invoice was on hold, regardless of how well they could navigate Oracle Payables.

I restructured the programme around a process-first, system-second principle — six role-based pathways (GL Accountant, AP Clerk, AR Manager, Fixed Asset Controller, Procurement Officer, Finance Business Partner) each built in four layers: process explainer video, Show Me Oracle navigation demonstration, Try Me sandboxed simulation at three levels of support, and scenario-based assessment drawn from real error patterns the SME interviews surfaced.

Results: 100% of 400+ users certified before go-live. 96% first-attempt pass rate across all six pathways. 62% reduction in support desk queries in the first 30 days post go-live — measured against a real baseline from the organisation's previous system rollout. The first Oracle implementation in the organisation's history to achieve full pre-go-live certification.
Accurate scoping is one of the most underrated skills in instructional design — it is where projects are won or lost before a single screen is built. My approach starts with three questions: what is the modality mix, what is the content complexity, and what is the review cycle structure?

Modality mix determines the production effort — a 30-minute eLearning module takes very different effort from a 30-minute ILT programme. Content complexity determines the discovery and storyboarding effort — highly regulated or technically specialised content (Oracle Financials, pharma, financial compliance) requires significantly more SME engagement and review cycles than process training. Review cycle structure determines the revision effort — a client with three levels of review adds more time than a client with one.

For large bids, I apply a must-know/need-to-know scope filter to every content area before producing an estimate — because the single most reliable way to reduce a project estimate is to reduce what gets included, not to underestimate the effort required to produce each element. On the Oracle project, applying this filter reduced the scope by 38% and made the four-month timeline genuinely achievable.

I also build a contingency assumption into every estimate for a new client — typically 15–20% — to cover the SME availability gaps, review delays, and scope clarifications that are predictable even when their specific form is not.
This is one of the most common tensions in ERP training projects and it is structural, not personal — the implementation team's priority is a technically clean go-live; the training team's priority is a humanly ready go-live. Both are necessary. Neither is sufficient alone.

The most productive framing I have found is to make the training team the implementation team's ally in the go-live readiness argument — rather than a competing workstream. When I present a go-live readiness dashboard showing that 78% of users have achieved certification in their Oracle role but 22% have not, I am giving the implementation lead information they need to make the go-live decision, not competing with them.

The specific conflict in CS7 was mid-project Oracle configuration changes — the implementation team making changes to approval hierarchies and Chart of Accounts structures that directly affected completed training content. The resolution was a configuration freeze policy — a formally agreed date after which training-relevant configuration changes required a documented change request. That made the conflict explicit, gave both teams a clear process for managing it, and eliminated the informal scope creep that would otherwise have consumed the training timeline.
A design proposal for a complex ERP training project needs to do four things: demonstrate that you understand the client's specific context (not just ERP training in general), propose a solution that is precisely scoped to that context, justify the instructional approach with evidence, and give the client confidence that the production timeline and budget are realistic.

My proposals are structured around five sections. First — context and problem statement: what is the implementation, who are the users, what are the go-live risks if training fails? Second — design approach: process-first or navigation-first, role-based or function-based, what modality mix and why? Third — scope: how many modules, which Oracle functions, what is included and what is explicitly excluded? Fourth — production plan: timeline, milestones, dependencies (Oracle sandbox availability, SME access, configuration freeze date), and team structure. Fifth — measurement: how go-live readiness will be defined, measured, and reported.

The element clients find most valuable — and most differentiated — is the explicit scope exclusion list. Stating clearly what the training will not cover, and why, demonstrates that the designer has made deliberate decisions rather than producing a generic response. It also protects the project from scope creep.
Development planning starts with an honest assessment of where each designer is and where the business needs them to go — and those two things are not always the same. I run a skills mapping exercise at the start of each year (or when joining a new team) that looks at three dimensions: instructional design craft (scenario writing, assessment design, modality selection, learning theory application), production capability (Storyline, Rise, Captivate, video — and at what level of proficiency), and consulting skills (stakeholder management, scoping, facilitation, client communication).

From that mapping, I identify each designer's development priority — the one capability that, if improved, would most increase their value on the projects they are working on. Then I design the development intervention around that priority: a stretch assignment on a live project, a pairing with a more experienced colleague, a focused external course, or a self-directed exploration with a prototype challenge and peer review.

The most important element is that the development plan is built into the work rather than sitting alongside it. A designer who is developing scenario-writing capability does so by writing scenarios on real projects, with structured feedback, not by attending a scenario-writing workshop in isolation.
Random audits create fear when the standard being audited against is unclear or subjective — when designers do not know what "good" looks like until a manager tells them their work is wrong. The antidote is a publicly shared, objectively defined quality standard that every designer helped create.

When I audit, I audit against a checklist that the team has already seen and agreed to — covering instructional accuracy, learning objective alignment, assessment validity, interaction logic, and visual consistency. The audit result is not a judgment of the designer — it is a quality measurement against an agreed standard. The designer who scores below the standard gets specific, actionable feedback based on the checklist criteria, not a general observation that their work is substandard.

I also make audit findings visible at team level — not to shame individuals, but to identify patterns. If multiple designers are producing assessments that test recall rather than performance, that is a team-level capability gap that needs a team-level response, not individual feedback. The audit is a diagnostic tool. Its purpose is to improve the system, not to evaluate the person.
The project management triangle — cost, quality, schedule — is real, and the honest answer is that you can optimise any two at the expense of the third. The key is making that trade-off decision explicitly, with the client, rather than absorbing it silently and hoping the team can manage it.

Across multiple concurrent projects, I use three mechanisms. First, a shared resource tracker that shows total team capacity against committed project hours — making overcommitment visible before it becomes a delivery problem. Second, a standardised scoping process that connects timeline to scope before production begins — if the timeline compresses, scope reduces; if scope expands, timeline extends or resource increases. Third, a quality floor — a minimum standard below which I will not allow work to be delivered, regardless of timeline pressure. The quality floor is protected by the design framework: shared templates, quality checklists, and calibration sessions mean that maintaining minimum quality does not require additional review time.

On the Oracle project, all three were essential: the configuration freeze policy protected the timeline, the must-know scope filter protected the cost, and the shared design framework protected the quality — simultaneously, across a team of ten.
Three technologies, in order of near-term impact:

AI-powered Digital Adoption Platforms — tools like WalkMe are already overlaying Oracle Financials Cloud with in-application guidance. The next generation will use AI to detect user behaviour patterns and proactively surface guidance before the user makes an error, rather than responding after. This will fundamentally change the post-go-live support model — reducing support desk queries not through better pre-go-live training but through better in-application assistance.

Generative AI for content production — AI will significantly reduce the time required to produce scenario-based assessment items, narration scripts, and process explainer content. The instructional design value will shift from content production to content curation and quality assurance — knowing which AI-generated scenarios are instructionally sound and which are not. This is a design skill, not a production skill, which favours experienced IDs over junior ones.

Simulation environments for Oracle practice — Oracle Cloud's quarterly update cycle makes screen-recording-based simulations expensive to maintain. The future is AI-generated Oracle practice environments that adapt to configuration changes automatically, reducing the rebuild cost that currently makes sandboxed simulation the most expensive element of ERP training production.
My pre-sales contribution spans three stages, each requiring different thinking.

At the RFI stage, I focus on positioning — understanding what the client is actually asking, what their real concern is, and whether our capability genuinely maps to their need. The best RFI responses do not answer every question generically; they demonstrate understanding of the client's specific context in a way that generic competitors cannot match.

At the RFP stage, I lead the instructional design section of the proposal — translating the client's requirements into a specific solution approach, with a rationale for every design decision. I also produce the scope and effort estimates that underpin the commercial proposal. Underestimating at RFP stage wins the bid and loses the client relationship. Accurate scoping wins the bid and builds the relationship.

At the contract and solutioning stage, I lead the initial client workshops — the needs analysis conversations that validate the RFP assumptions and surface the information we did not have when we wrote the proposal. This is where the design rationale is stress-tested and where the production plan is finalised. In my experience at Ozemio, this stage is where the value of pre-sales experience is most visible — clients who feel heard and understood in the solutioning conversation sign contracts faster and with fewer scope disputes.
